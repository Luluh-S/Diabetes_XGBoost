EDA · 1 of 3 · The crowd

Meet the crowd.

223,243 adults from the CDC’s 2021 phone survey, shrunk to 200. Each dot stands for about 1,100 real people.

The red dots have diabetes: 30 of 200, about one in seven.

Why it matters: about 1 in 5 US adults with diabetes don’t know they have it (CDC). A free survey could point them to a blood test.

This is our data: the CDC BRFSS 2021 survey. We started with 236,378 people and removed 13,135 exact duplicate rows, so the same answers cannot sit in both the training and the test set. That leaves 223,243 people, each with 21 answers and no missing values. 15% have diabetes. On screen, each dot is about 1,100 people. Why does this matter? The CDC estimates that about 1 in 5 US adults with diabetes, 8.7 million people, do not know they have it. A blood test finds them, but only if they get one. If 21 survey questions can point the right people to a test, that is a free first step. So our mission is: find the 30.

FOUND 5 / 30

XGBoost · 1 of 3 · The trap

Say “no” to everyone: 85% right.

Only 15% have diabetes, so a model that never says yes is right 85% of the time. It finds none of the 30.

Here is the trap. A model that answers no for everyone is 85% accurate and finds nobody: the hollow dots are the people it misses. A plain XGBoost model is not much better: it flags 8 people and finds only 5 of the 30, a recall of 15%. So accuracy is the wrong score for us. We judge by recall, how many of the 30 we find, and by precision, how many flags are right.

FOUND 19 / 30

XGBoost · 2 of 3 · Make them count

Make the 30 count.

In training, each diabetes case counts 5.7 times. The model then ranks everyone, and the cut-off decides who gets flagged.

Two fixes. First, class weights: each diabetes case counts 5.7 times in training, the ratio of no to yes. Second, the cut-off. The model gives everyone a score and lines them up, most likely on the left. Everyone left of the line is flagged. Move the line right and we find more people, with more false alarms. We tuned the model with a 5-fold grid search (400 trees, depth 3, learning rate 0.05) and picked the cut-off with the best F1 on cross-validation: 0.62. For screening, 0.48 finds 24 of the 30 but flags 80 people.

ROC-AUC · how well it ranks

These are the test results, on 44,649 people the model never saw. Out of 6,679 people with diabetes it found 4,238: recall 0.63. The price is false alarms: about 2 of every 3 flags are wrong, precision 0.37. ROC-AUC is 0.814, the same as in cross-validation, so the model is not overfitting. The answer it leans on most is high blood pressure: 59% of the model’s gain. And the 11 we miss? They gave low-risk answers: many feel fine and report no high blood pressure. That is the honest limit of a survey, and why the flag only sends people to a blood test, it does not replace one.

The same 200 people. Now it is Gradient Boosting’s turn.

Our second model is Gradient Boosting from scikit-learn. It uses the same 80/20 split and is tested on the same 44,649 people, so the comparison is fair. Without balancing it falls into the same trap: 85.5% accuracy but only 16% of diabetics found. So we balanced the training data by undersampling: we kept all 26,716 people with diabetes and the same number of random people without. Settings: 300 trees, learning rate 0.05, depth 4, and each tree sees 80% of the rows. Finally we picked the cut-off on a validation part of the training data, the same way as for XGBoost.

Gradient Boosting · 2 of 3 · The cut-off

Its cut-off: 0.63.

Picked on validation data for the best F1. At the default 0.5 it flags far more people: recall 0.79, but precision only 0.31.

Same idea as XGBoost: the model ranks everyone, and the cut-off decides who is flagged. Trained on 50/50 data, the scores run high, so 0.5 flags too many people: it finds 79% of diabetics, but only 31% of its flags are right. The cut-off with the best F1 on validation data is 0.63. In our crowd of 200 it flags 51 people and finds 19 of the 30.

Comparison · 3 of 3 · The verdict

Our pick

XGBoost.

A near tie on every score.

XGBoost and Gradient Boosting reach ROC-AUC 0.814, Random Forest 0.810. All three find 19 of the 30.

XGBoost trains fastest.

0.35 s, against 0.85 s for Random Forest and 8.2 s for Gradient Boosting.

The data sets the limit.

Every model we tried, from logistic regression to a neural network, stops near 0.815. 65 more CDC survey columns lifted it to 0.829.

Our pick is XGBoost. The scores are almost a tie, so we choose on everything else: XGBoost is a little ahead of Random Forest, trains fastest, handles class imbalance with a single setting and handles missing answers on its own. The bigger lesson is the third point. With 21 answers every model we tried stops between 0.80 and 0.815 ROC-AUC: logistic regression 0.806, Random Forest 0.810, a small neural network 0.811, Gradient Boosting and CatBoost 0.814, LightGBM 0.815. Published studies on this survey report the same range, about 0.79 to 0.83. When we rebuilt the data from the original CDC survey and added 65 more columns, such as kidney disease, arthritis, depression, exact age and alcohol per week, XGBoost rose to 0.829 and F1 to 0.484. We left out anything caused by a diagnosis, like medicines and insulin. So the limit was the information, not the model.
